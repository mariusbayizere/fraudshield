\section{Generalisation: nulls, held-out fraud types and one failure}
\label{sec:generalisation}

Once a single engineered feature separated the classes at \nGateFloor{} on the evaluation draw's
test rows (Section~\ref{sec:floor}), the headline AUC could no longer show that a model had learned anything a
one-line rule could not.
Leave-one-country-out and a fraud sub-variant that appears only in the test period were meant to
carry that weight instead. Both are null. This section reports them as null results rather than
dropping them, gives our inference about why this benchmark could not make them informative, and
reports the one experiment that did find a failure.

\subsection{Leave-one-country-out}

Each country is removed from training entirely and then scored, and each result is compared with
the same country scored by the model that saw it (Table~\ref{tab:loco}). Removing a country costs
at most \nLocoMaxLoss{} AUC. In the earlier battery on draw \texttt{6abde44e}, made before the
encoding correction described below, it cost at most \nEarlyLocoMaxLoss{}.

A test checks that no fraud parameter in the generator's \texttt{fraud} configuration is keyed by
country code. The test's own description is that country enters an incident only as a lookup for
which mule, merchant, agent, currency or time zone it uses, so that withholding a country withholds
no fraud mechanism; that description is not itself tested. Country does change the fraud through
some path the test does not cover: CD's test-period fraud rate is \nCoFraudRateCD{}, against
\nCoFraudRateMin{} to \nCoFraudRateMax{} for the other countries (Table~\ref{tab:slices}). We infer
that the null follows from the absence of country-keyed fraud mechanisms; no experiment isolates it.
Country-level generalisation cannot be evaluated on this benchmark and belongs to a real-data
validation plan. We did not change the generator to make countries differ: no source read gives
fraud prevalence by type and country for these markets, so the only way to choose such a parameter
would be to pick whatever makes the experiment show a gap, which defines the ground truth from the
test's desired answer (ADR 0028).

\subsection{A novel variant held out in time}

\texttt{novel\_esim\_delayed\_drain} occurs only in the test period. All variants are scored
against the same legitimate rows at one threshold, the \nFPRBudget{} false-positive budget over all
legitimate rows. The unseen variant is caught at \nNovelRecall{} \nNovelCI{} (\nNovelCaught{} of
\nNovelRows{}), against \nBaseVarRecall{} \nBaseVarCI{} for the base scenarios the model trained
on: at least as often as the familiar shape. Those \nNovelRows{} rows sit on only \nNovelAccounts{}
accounts, so they are far from independent; an account-clustered bootstrap gives \nCluNovelci{},
and at a recall this close to one neither it nor the Wilson interval describes the uncertainty well.
In the earlier battery the figures were
\nEarlyNovelRecall{} against \nEarlyBaseRecall{}. The variant differs from the base SIM-swap
scenario in its enabling event (recorded as a device change rather than a SIM swap), in its delay
(the drain follows that event by days; the base scenario's lead is drawn from a long-tailed
distribution with a median of \nLeadMedianMinutes{} minutes), and in its channel (bank transfer for
customers with a smartphone). Its transaction pattern is still a multi-row incident. We infer that a
model that detects such incidents catches it without having seen it; no attribution or ablation was
run on these rows. This is not an out-of-distribution test.

fewer than the test period's \nTestFraud{} observed frauds. Table~\ref{tab:reconcile} accounts for
all of them from the dataset's own labels: the difference is label noise, legitimate rows that the
observed label marks as fraud, which carry no scenario and so no variant. The table also counts the

\subsection{Leave-one-fraud-type-out}
\label{sec:lofto}

Each of the generator's \nScenarios{} fraud scenarios was removed in turn from every fitted set of
the full-volume model (training, validation, calibration and the target encoding), and the refitted
model was scored on the whole test period (PB-66; \texttt{docs/benchmarks/lofto\_d8083dbc.txt}).
\textbf{This measures what never fitting on a scenario costs, not what never having seen it costs}:
the other rows' features were computed over a history that still contains the held-out scenario's
rows, through velocity, counterparty novelty and device sharing. Like the rest of PB-66's run it was
pre-specified in repository history and is not blind: the test period had been scored
\nPriorReads{} times before, \nPriorReadsFull{} of them in full.

The owner's hypothesis, with numeric ranges confirmed before the run, was that scenarios sharing
burst or counterparty-novelty structure (velocity, SIM swap and mule) keep their recall when held out,
and that single-row scenarios (agent, card-not-present and merchant) drop clearly: a drop of at least
\nLoRangeSingleDrop{} with the held-out interval's upper bound below the production recall.
Table~\ref{tab:lofto} reports each against its range. \textbf{The hypothesis is partly supported}, in the pre-specified rule's term.
The burst scenarios keep their recall. Of the single-row scenarios only agent fraud drops
clearly. Card-not-present does not; merchant fraud's held-out recall is within its bound of
\nLoRangeSingle{}, but its drop is smaller than \nLoRangeSingleDrop{}, so it does not drop clearly
either; both are refuted as stated. PB-66's own ranges for the other scenarios fare no better.
Account takeover keeps its recall above \nLoRangeAto{}, as predicted, but loses more than the range
allowed, so that prediction is partly confirmed. Synthetic identity's recall falls by
\nLoSynDrop{}, refuting its range on both counts. At the flag threshold every row except account
takeover and synthetic identity loses more than at the budget; we infer that a calibration fitted
without a scenario puts much of it below the threshold even where the ranking still separates it.

\subsection{What the nulls have in common}

We infer a different explanation for each null. The country null is the one the test above
supports: no fraud parameter is keyed by country. The novel-variant null and the redundancy of
Section~\ref{sec:redundancy} we infer follow from incident structure: several of the generator's
scenarios stage fraud as multi-row incidents, and the novel variant and the
\nGroupsAboveCeiling{} strongest feature groups are views of that structure.
Not every scenario is multi-row (agent, card-not-present and merchant incidents can be a single row,
Appendix~\ref{app:scenarios}), and the agent and corridor groups alone barely separate the classes
(Table~\ref{tab:groups}). No experiment varies the incident structure, so this explanation is an
inference. What the benchmark supports is detection on its own scenarios, and, for one booster on one
shared GitHub-hosted runner, the median cost of explaining that detection. It supports no claim about generalisation, geographic or temporal.

\subsection{A pre-specified non-burst variant: the production model's one failure on a variant}

A generalisation test needs a mechanism that actually differs, and the ``sent by mistake'' reversal
scam is one. Its ingredients are documented among Kenyan M-PESA users. In a survey of them,
forged M-PESA confirmation messages were the most common scam reported
\cite[Section~5.1.1]{dogan2025grift}; the same study notes that M-PESA's business services block
reversals to foil opportunistic and malicious reversal requests \cite[Section~2]{dogan2025grift},
and one respondent reported asking the sender to reverse an apparently mistaken transfer
\cite[Section~5.3.3]{dogan2025grift}. The scam itself is described in the Kenyan press: the victim
receives a fake M-PESA message that shows ``LOCKED'' where the balance should be, the scammer claims
to have sent the money by mistake, often with an emotional story, and the victim sends it back, only
to find that no money was ever deposited; Safaricom's advice, quoted there, is not to refund the
money \cite{star2025mpesa}. We read the victim's loss as a single transfer that the victim
initiates, to the number of the claimed sender; the press account says the victim sends the money
back, not to which number. Neither source says whether the victim has paid that
number before; since it is the scammer's own number, we read the documented form as a transfer to a
new counterparty.

The benchmark's variant keeps the single, victim-initiated transfer and sends it to a counterparty
already on the victim's own payee list. \textbf{That recipient is our modelling choice, not a property
of the documented scam: ADR 0028, and the justification committed with the variant and its predicted recall
(\prespec{656d24e}{2026-09-22}{6bc842d}{2026-09-22}, Section~\ref{sec:discussion}), cite the scam
typology for it, and the sources cited do not support it.} The variant therefore departs from the
documented form in its recipient, and ADR 0028 has been corrected to say so. The variant also has its own amount rule: amounts are drawn around \nRevAmountMultiplier{} times
the channel's median legitimate amount, and pass through the same below-threshold adaptation as other
fraud. A test checks that each row is a single transaction in the test period to
a counterparty on the payee list, but a listed counterparty need not have been paid before.
Measured on the regenerated draw with complete history,
\texttt{counterparty\_is\_new\_for\_account} is \textbf{true on \nRevNovelScored{} of the
\nRevFraud{} rows scored as fraud}: their payee was on the list but had never been paid by that
account. It is false on the other \nRevNotNovelScored{}, \nRevPriorThreePlus{} of which had paid
the same counterparty at least \nRevPriorThreshold{} times before
(\texttt{evidence/\allowbreak reversal\_\allowbreak novelty\_\allowbreak d8083dbc.txt}). The
variant removes burst structure from every row, but counterparty novelty only from
\nRevNotNovelScored{} of the \nRevFraud{}. The generator stages each such row as a \texttt{mule\_account} row
with its own sub-variant label (Table~\ref{tab:scenarios}).

The variant's definition, its justification and the predicted result were committed before the
dataset was regenerated and before any measurement existed (ADR 0028). The prediction: recall at
the same threshold \textbf{between \nRevPredLow{} and \nRevPredHigh{}}, lower than the base
scenarios but with some of the variant still caught, with recall below about \nRevPredFloor{} stated as the outcome that would
say burst structure and counterparty novelty carry nearly all of the benchmark's detection power.

\textbf{Result.} \texttt{reversal\_scam\_social\_engineering} is caught at \nRevRecall{}
(\nRevCaught{} of \nRevFraud{}, Wilson \nRevCI{}), against \nBaseVarRecall{} \nBaseVarCI{} for the
base scenarios on \nBaseVarFraud{} rows; its AUC is \nRevAUC{}. The variant's \nRevFraud{} rows are on
\nRevAccounts{} different accounts, so row-level dependence does not affect its interval (an
account-clustered bootstrap gives \nCluRevci{}); the base scenarios' rows sit on \nBaseAccounts{}
accounts, and clustering gives \nCluBaseci{}. Its AUC of \nRevAUC{} means the variant is partly ranked even
though it is rarely flagged. The recall intervals do not overlap. The denominator is \nRevFraud{}
rather than the \nRevRows{} rows the generator wrote because the evaluation reads the observed
label, as a deployed model would: all \nRevTrueFraud{} rows are in the test period and are fraud by
their true label, and \nRevScoredUnlabelled{} of them carry the benchmark's missed-fraud label noise
(Section~\ref{sec:dataset}), so they are scored as legitimate rows
(\texttt{evidence/\allowbreak reversal\_\allowbreak labels\_\allowbreak d8083dbc.txt},
Appendix~\ref{app:evidence}). The point estimate is below the pre-specified range; the interval
reaches past the range's lower edge, so ``below the predicted range'' holds for the point estimate
and not for the whole interval.

\textbf{A correction, reported rather than hidden.} The first measurement gave \nRevRecallFirst{}
\nRevCIFirst{} (\nRevCaughtFirst{} of \nRevFraud{}; AUC \nRevAUCFirst{}) against
\nBaseVarRecallFirst{} \nBaseVarCIFirst{}. It used a random-fold target encoding, which the project's
rule E1 forbids (every fold, split, sample and target encoding groups by the feature's unit of
history); re-running on the same data with the permitted encoding produced the figures above. With
the first figures the whole interval lay below the predicted range; with the corrected ones only the
point estimate does, so the conclusion narrowed.

\textbf{The production ensemble, row by row.} The figures above are the diagnostic model's. The
production ensemble was scored on the same rows with its per-row scores kept
(\texttt{evidence/\allowbreak round2\_\allowbreak gate\_\allowbreak d8083dbc.txt},
\texttt{evidence/\allowbreak round2\_\allowbreak full\_\allowbreak volume\_\allowbreak d8083dbc.txt}). \textbf{The counts are small,
and the counts come first.} At the \nFPRBudget{} budget the gate model catches \nGateRevAllBudgetCaught{}
of the \nGateRevAllBudgetRows{} reversal rows. All of them are rows whose recipient the account had
never paid: \nGateRevNewBudgetCaught{} of the \nGateRevNewBudgetRows{} such rows (recall
\nGateRevNewBudgetRecall{}, Wilson \nGateRevNewBudgetWilson{}, account-clustered
\nGateRevNewBudgetClustered{}), against \nGateRevKnownBudgetCaught{} of the
\nGateRevKnownBudgetRows{} rows whose recipient it had paid before (Wilson
\nGateRevKnownBudgetWilson{}). The full-volume model gives the same pattern:
\nFvRevNewBudgetCaught{} of \nFvRevNewBudgetRows{} never-paid rows (Wilson \nFvRevNewBudgetWilson{})
and \nFvRevKnownBudgetCaught{} of \nFvRevKnownBudgetRows{} previously paid rows. At the flag threshold
neither model catches any reversal row (\nGateRevAllFlagCaught{} of \nGateRevAllFlagRows{} and
\nFvRevAllFlagCaught{} of \nFvRevAllFlagRows{}). Each reversal row is on its own account, so the
clustered and Wilson intervals describe the same uncertainty in different ways; the clustered ones hold
the budget's threshold fixed at its full-sample value.

\textbf{What this does and does not show.} On a held-out shape, a variant with no burst structure,
paid to a listed counterparty and with its own amount rule, is rarely caught by models trained on
this benchmark's scenarios, and at the flag threshold not at all. Within the variant, every row the
production ensemble catches at the budget has a never-paid recipient, and none of the rows sent to a
payee the account had paid before is caught. Both groups share the variant's amount rule and its
lack of burst structure, so this points to counterparty novelty as what the model still detects in
it. It does not isolate novelty as a cause: the split is observational, not a designed comparison;
the groups were not assigned at random and may differ in other ways, such as the history their
accounts carry; and the counts are \nGateRevNewBudgetRows{} and \nGateRevKnownBudgetRows{}. We infer
that the absence of burst structure is a likely contributor to the miss overall. A variant built with
a new recipient on every row, as in the documented form, would test this directly and has not been
measured. The result concerns this one variant, not
non-burst fraud in general: other typologies are untested, and whether data this benchmark does not
contain, such as an institution's account and KYC records (ADR 0055), would help is not known.
\texttt{adapted\_below\_threshold}, a variant present in both training and test, rests on
\nAdaptFraud{} fraud rows (recall \nAdaptRecall{} \nAdaptCI{}); its interval overlaps the base
scenarios' and establishes nothing on its own.
