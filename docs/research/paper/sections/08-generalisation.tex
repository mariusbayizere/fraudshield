\section{Generalisation: nulls and one failure}
\label{sec:generalisation}

Once a single engineered feature separated the classes at \nGateFloor{} on the test rows, the
headline AUC could no longer show that a model had learned anything a one-line rule could not.
Leave-one-country-out and a fraud sub-variant that appears only in the test period were meant to
carry that weight instead. Both are null. This section reports them as null results rather than
dropping them, gives our inference about why this benchmark could not make them informative, and
reports the one experiment that did find a failure.

\subsection{Leave-one-country-out}

Each country is removed from training entirely and then scored, and each result is compared with
the same country scored by the model that saw it (Table~\ref{tab:loco}). Removing a country costs
at most \nLocoMaxLoss{} AUC. In the earlier battery on draw \texttt{6abde44e}, made before the
encoding correction described below, it cost at most \nEarlyLocoMaxLoss{}.

A test checks that no fraud parameter in the generator's \texttt{fraud} configuration is keyed by
country code. The test's own description is that country enters an incident only as a lookup for
which mule, merchant, agent, currency or time zone it uses, so that withholding a country withholds
no fraud mechanism; that description is not itself tested. Country does change the fraud through
some path the test does not cover: CD's test-period fraud rate is \nCoFraudRateCD{}, against
\nCoFraudRateMin{} to \nCoFraudRateMax{} for the other countries (Table~\ref{tab:slices}). We infer
that the null follows from the absence of country-keyed fraud mechanisms; no experiment isolates it.
Country-level generalisation cannot be evaluated on this benchmark and belongs to a real-data
validation plan. We did not change the generator to make countries differ: no source read gives
fraud prevalence by type and country for these markets, so the only way to choose such a parameter
would be to pick whatever makes the experiment show a gap, which defines the ground truth from the
test's desired answer (ADR 0028).

\subsection{A novel variant held out in time}

\texttt{novel\_esim\_delayed\_drain} occurs only in the test period. All variants are scored
against the same legitimate rows at one threshold, the \nFPRBudget{} false-positive budget over all
legitimate rows. The unseen variant is caught at \nNovelRecall{} \nNovelCI{} (\nNovelCaught{} of
\nNovelRows{}), against \nBaseVarRecall{} \nBaseVarCI{} for the base scenarios the model trained
on: at least as often as the familiar shape. In the earlier battery the figures were
\nEarlyNovelRecall{} against \nEarlyBaseRecall{}. The variant differs from the base SIM-swap
scenario in its enabling event (recorded as a device change rather than a SIM swap), in its delay
(the drain follows that event by days; the base scenario's lead is drawn from a long-tailed
distribution with a median of \nLeadMedianMinutes{} minutes), and in its channel (bank transfer for
customers with a smartphone). Its transaction pattern is still a multi-row incident. We infer that a
model that detects such incidents catches it without having seen it; no attribution or ablation was
run on these rows. This is not an out-of-distribution test.

\subsection{What the nulls have in common}

We infer one explanation for both nulls and for the redundancy of Section~\ref{sec:redundancy}:
several of the generator's scenarios stage fraud as multi-row incidents, and the countries, the novel
variant and the \nGroupsAboveCeiling{} strongest feature groups are each views of that structure.
Not every scenario is multi-row (agent, card-not-present and merchant incidents can be a single row,
Appendix~\ref{app:scenarios}), and the agent and corridor groups alone barely separate the classes
(Table~\ref{tab:groups}). No experiment varies the incident structure, so this explanation is an
inference. What the benchmark supports is detection on its own scenarios, and the cost of computing
and explaining that detection. It supports no claim about generalisation, geographic or temporal.

\subsection{A pre-registered non-burst variant: the one failure found}

A generalisation test needs a mechanism that actually differs, and the ``sent by mistake'' reversal
scam is one. Its ingredients are documented among Kenyan M-PESA users. In a survey of them,
forged M-PESA confirmation messages were the most common scam reported
\cite[Section~5.1.1]{dogan2025grift}; the same study notes that M-PESA's business services block
reversals to foil opportunistic and malicious reversal requests \cite[Section~2]{dogan2025grift},
and one respondent reported asking the sender to reverse an apparently mistaken transfer
\cite[Section~5.3.3]{dogan2025grift}. The scam itself is described in the Kenyan press: the victim
receives a fake M-PESA message that shows ``LOCKED'' where the balance should be, the scammer claims
to have sent the money by mistake, often with an emotional story, and the victim sends it back, only
to find that no money was ever deposited; Safaricom's advice, quoted there, is not to refund the
money \cite{star2025mpesa}. The victim's loss is therefore a single transfer that the victim
initiates, to the number of the claimed sender. Neither source says whether the victim has paid that
number before; since it is the scammer's own number, we read the documented form as a transfer to a
new counterparty.

The benchmark's variant keeps the single, victim-initiated transfer and departs from that form in its
recipient: a counterparty already on the victim's own payee list. ADR 0028, and the justification
committed at pre-registration, state that this property comes from the typology; neither source we
read supports that, so it is a modelling choice, and the variant departs from the documented scam in
that respect. The variant also has its own amount rule: \nRevAmountMultiplier{} times the channel's
median legitimate amount. A test checks that each row is a single transaction in the test period to
a counterparty on the payee list. Whether \texttt{counterparty\_is\_new\_for\_account} is in fact false
on these rows was not measured: a listed counterparty may not have been paid before. So the variant
removes burst structure by construction and counterparty novelty by construction of the payee list,
not as verified on the feature. The generator stages each such row as a \texttt{mule\_account} row
with its own sub-variant label (Table~\ref{tab:scenarios}).

The variant's definition, its justification and the predicted result were committed before the
dataset was regenerated and before any measurement existed (ADR 0028). The prediction: recall at
the same threshold \textbf{between \nRevPredLow{} and \nRevPredHigh{}}, lower than the base
scenarios but not zero, with recall below about \nRevPredFloor{} stated as the outcome that would
say burst structure and counterparty novelty carry nearly all of the benchmark's detection power.

\textbf{Result.} \texttt{reversal\_scam\_social\_engineering} is caught at \nRevRecall{}
(\nRevCaught{} of \nRevFraud{}, Wilson \nRevCI{}), against \nBaseVarRecall{} \nBaseVarCI{} for the
base scenarios on \nBaseVarFraud{} rows; its AUC is \nRevAUC{}, so the variant is partly ranked even
though it is rarely flagged. The recall intervals do not overlap. The denominator is \nRevFraud{}
rather than the \nRevRows{} rows the generator wrote because the evaluation reads the observed
label, as a deployed model would: all \nRevTrueFraud{} rows are in the test period and are fraud by
their true label, and \nRevScoredUnlabelled{} of them carry the benchmark's missed-fraud label noise
(Section~\ref{sec:dataset}), so they are scored as legitimate rows
(\texttt{evidence/\allowbreak reversal\_\allowbreak labels\_\allowbreak d8083dbc.txt},
Appendix~\ref{app:evidence}). The point estimate is below the pre-registered range; the interval
reaches past the range's lower edge, so ``below the predicted range'' holds for the point estimate
and not for the whole interval.

\textbf{A correction, reported rather than hidden.} The first measurement gave \nRevRecallFirst{}
\nRevCIFirst{} (\nRevCaughtFirst{} of \nRevFraud{}; AUC \nRevAUCFirst{}) against
\nBaseVarRecallFirst{} \nBaseVarCIFirst{}. It used a random-fold target encoding, which the project's
rule E1 forbids (every fold, split, sample and target encoding groups by the feature's unit of
history); re-running on the same data with the permitted encoding produced the figures above. With
the first figures the whole interval lay below the predicted range; with the corrected ones only the
point estimate does, so the conclusion narrowed.

\textbf{What this does and does not show.} On a held-out shape, a variant with no burst structure,
paid to a listed counterparty and with its own amount rule, is rarely caught by a model trained on
this benchmark's scenarios. Because the variant differs from them in several respects at once, the
result cannot say which of them causes the miss; we infer that the absence of burst structure and of
counterparty novelty is the main cause, without a measurement that isolates it. A variant with a new
recipient, as in the documented form, would separate those causes and has not been measured; whether it
would be easier or harder to catch is not established. The result concerns this one variant, not
non-burst fraud in general: other typologies are untested, and whether data this benchmark does not
contain, such as an institution's account and KYC records (ADR 0055), would help is not known.
\texttt{adapted\_below\_threshold}, a variant present in both training and test, rests on
\nAdaptFraud{} fraud rows (recall \nAdaptRecall{} \nAdaptCI{}); its interval overlaps the base
scenarios' and establishes nothing on its own.
