The recall miss is recorded as a finding at this training volume, with the threshold unchanged and

so it first reaches \nTargetRecall{} somewhere between those rates, at more than the budget
the specification's own recall-at-budget metric allows.

\paragraph{Seed variance.} The specification claims that the ensemble reduces variance by
\nSpecVarianceClaim{} against a single model; the seed-variance evidence file records that the
claim carries no citation (claims register, C-6).
The measurement is of the \emph{uncalibrated} weighted combination on the diagnostic battery's rows,
over \nSeeds{} seeds: its seed-to-seed AUC standard deviation is \nSeedSdEnsemble{}, against
\nSeedSdXGB{} for XGBoost alone and \nSeedSdLGB{} for LightGBM alone. Computed from the per-seed
AUCs, the combination's standard deviation is \nSeedSdRelXGB{} relative to XGBoost's, higher and so
no reduction, and \nSeedSdRelLGB{} relative to LightGBM's. (The evidence file prints the same
comparisons as reductions, \nSeedSdDiffXGB{} and \nSeedSdDiffLGB{}, from the standard deviations
as it rounds them.) We infer, without
having computed an interval for the ratio, that \nSeeds{} seeds cannot distinguish standard
deviations this close, so neither comparison supports the claim as a property of the ensemble. It is a different quantity from the calibrated ensemble's seed spread on the gate rows,
\nGateSeedAUC{}, reported above.

\subsection{Training volume}
\label{sec:volume}

The declared gate model trains on \nTrainUsed{} of the \nTrainRows{} training-period rows, so the recall
miss could have been a matter of training volume. A learning curve tested that (PB-67). The lab notebook
records its design, its prediction and the rule for reading it as written before any size was run
(the commit was rebased after the outcome, so the history cannot confirm that order;
Table~\ref{tab:predictions}): the same model, thresholds, seeds and resamples as the gate, the whole test period at every
size, and validation and calibration splits of the gate's size. The design changed before any model
was fitted, because the development machine could not build the planned shared feature cache: the
\nLcTrainA{}, \nLcTrainB{} and \nLcTrainC{} points come from one cache and the largest from its own
\nLcCorpusLarge{}-row corpus. The change the notebook records as made before any result named a
\nLcCorpusPlanned{}-row corpus for the shared cache; the cache was built from \nLcCorpusSmall{} rows.
The evidence files show the smaller points all fitted on that trimmed cache, so the trim preceded
every fit, but it was written down only with the first results. The last step therefore changes the depth of the corpus as well as
the volume.

The rule the notebook records as written before the runs (its commit was rebased after the outcome,
so the history cannot confirm that order; Table~\ref{tab:predictions}) was that the miss is a property of the benchmark and model if the step from
\nLcTrainC{} to \nLcTrainD{} rows gains less than the half-width of the largest size's interval and
that interval's upper bound stays below \nTargetRecall{}. Both hold: the step is \nLcStepCD{} at seed 1
(\nLcSeedStepCD{} on the means over seeds), and the largest size's interval, \nLcRecallciD{}, ends
below the target. No interval at any size reaches the target; the highest upper bound of
either kind is \nLcMaxUpper{}. Because that last step also changes the corpus, the cleaner evidence is the
\nLcVolumeFactorAC{}-fold step within one cache, from \nLcTrainA{} to \nLcTrainC{} rows: recall moves
\nLcStepAC{} at seed 1 and \nLcSeedGainAC{} on the means over seeds, less than the half-width of any
seed-1 interval on the curve. Across the whole \nLcVolumeFactor{}-fold range the mean over seeds

while every interval stays below the target.
Clustering the intervals by account changes none of this: each clustered interval is barely wider
than its row interval, and every one still ends below the target.

\textbf{Full volume.} PB-66 then trained the same design on the whole training period,
\nFvTrainRows{} rows, about \nFullVolumeFactor{} times the gate's \nTrainUsed{}, under a rule fixed
before that run (its pre-specification, \texttt{docs/research/preregistration\_pb66\_full\_volume.md}):
volume would be said to move recall past the \nLcTrainD{}-row point only if the full-volume seed-1
interval lay entirely above \nFvRuleBound{} and the mean over \nSeeds{} seeds exceeded \nFvRuleMean{} by more
than \nFvRuleMargin{}. Neither holds: the seed-1 interval is \nFvRecallSixci{} and the mean
\nFvSeedRecallSix{}. The rule was stated knowing it could not be met inside PB-66's own prediction:
it needs a mean above \nFvRuleNeeded{}, and the predicted range topped out at \nFvPredTop{}. Full
volume also changes more than volume: the validation and calibration splits grow, and
\nFvTruncated{} of the training rows (a share of \nFvTruncatedShare{}) fall within
\nFvTruncatedDays{} days of the draw's first day, so their windowed history is truncated (PB-76).
\textbf{At every volume measured, up to all \nFvTrainRows{} training rows of this draw, and for this
model, the recall shortfall is not closed by training volume.} Whether a different model, or features
that do not depend on corpus depth, would reach the target is not tested.

The prediction was partly wrong, and is reported with the others in Table~\ref{tab:predictions}.
It said recall at the flag threshold would rise to between \nLcPredLow{} and \nLcPredHigh{} at the
largest size; it landed in that range, without rising at seed 1 and rising on
the means, each step within a standard deviation. It said recall at the \nFPRBudget{} budget would rise to between \nLcPredBudgetLow{} and
\nLcPredBudgetHigh{}; at seed 1 it read \nLcBudgetRecallD{} \nLcBudgetRecallciD{}, below the range
(at \nLcTrainC{} rows it had read \nLcBudgetRecallC{}), and over \nSeeds{} seeds $\nLcSeedBudgetD$,
inside it. The budget figures compare weakly across sizes: the false-positive rate realised at the
budget's threshold was \nLcRealFprA{}, \nLcRealFprB{}, \nLcRealFprC{} and \nLcRealFprD{} at the
sizes in turn, so each size is read at a different operating point. We infer that ties (rows with the same calibrated score straddling the threshold) in the
isotonic calibration cause the spread; the evidence files record the realised rate, not its cause.

At seed 1, the curve's smallest point also differs from the gate run at the same \nTrainUsed{}
rows, by more than the curve's own range. They are different selections of training rows, which
is also why the curve's \nLcTrainA{} rows hold \nLcFraudA{} frauds and the gate's \nGateTrainFraud{}. That comparison concerns which rows are used rather than how
many, and Section~\ref{sec:rowselection} reports it with its limits.

\label{sec:redundancy}

\textbf{The diagnostic model is untuned, and overfit where it was measured; that bounds every
ablation claim.} The velocity group alone scores \nGroupVelocity{}, below one of its own members,
\texttt{velocity\_ratio\_1h\_vs\_30d}, which ranks the same test rows at \nVelRatioAlone{} on its
own. Refitting the velocity-only booster and reading it at each boosting round shows why: its test
AUC peaks at \nVelPeak{} near round \nVelPeakRound{} of those read and falls to \nVelAtRounds{} by
round \nVelRounds{}, where the battery stops; weighting the classes gives \nVelWeightedAtRounds{}
(\texttt{evidence/\allowbreak clustered\_\allowbreak intervals\_\allowbreak d8083dbc.txt}). The
model has no early stopping and no tuning, and on the velocity group it overfits; we infer the same
for the other groups, where it was not measured. So, we infer, each group-alone figure is a lower
bound on what that group can do with a better-fitted model, and the leave-one-group-out drops are
differences between overfitted models whose direction of error is not known. The redundancy finding
survives this, because it rests on lower bounds: at their account-clustered lower bounds
(\nCluCounterpartyLow{}, \nCluVelocityLow{}, \nCluTemporalLow{} and \nCluGeographicLow{}),
\nGroupsAboveCeilingClu{} groups still exceed the \nCeiling{} ceiling alone. What does not survive is
any ranking of groups by importance, and any reading of a small leave-one-group-out drop as a group
being unimportant.

On the diagnostic model, several groups each separate the classes alone, so a
leave-one-group-out ablation reports each as nearly free. This is a result about how this generator
encodes signal. We infer, without having isolated it, that a generator that plants fraud as
multi-row incidents writes the same structure into the velocity, counterparty, temporal and
geographic views at once. In an earlier battery on draw \texttt{6abde44e}, with
\nEarlyHeldOutRows{} test rows and made before the encoding correction of
Section~\ref{sec:generalisation}, the same groups each reached at least \nEarlyGroupMin{}; the
threshold moved between the runs and the finding did not, and the move cannot be attributed to any
one of the differences between them.

It also disposes of a planned experiment, although that experiment produces the largest ablation
effect in this paper. The specification claims that models built elsewhere reach far lower AUC on
East African mobile money data; the claim is unverified (claims register, C-4), and the proposed
replacement was to compare a card-style feature set with the full set. Restricting the production
ensemble to the groups we assume a card-fraud model would carry (velocity, amount, temporal,
geographic, device and account profile), which drops the counterparty, agent, corridor and
synthetic-identity groups, changes AUC by \nAblCardD{}, to \nAblCardAUC{} (paired account-clustered
interval \nAblCardDiffci{}), and recall at the \nFPRBudget{} budget from \nGateRecallFPR{} to
\nAblCardR{}.
Its paired clustered interval excludes no change on this draw and seed, and its size is exactly why it
cannot answer the claim. The
dropped groups include counterparty, the strongest group on its own (\nGroupCounterparty{}) and the
one whose removal costs most, \nGroupMaxDrop{} on the diagnostic model, and the generator writes counterparty
structure into its scenarios by design: SIM-swap drains go to new or mule counterparties, and mule
accounts collect many inbound transfers (Appendix~\ref{app:scenarios}). We infer that the drop
measures how much of the fraud this generator plants is carried by the dropped groups, chiefly
counterparty structure, which is a consequence of assumed scenario parameters; the ablation removes
the groups together and does not separate them. The claim is about a change of population,
a model built on other transactions and applied to East African ones, and a feature subset trained
and tested on one synthetic population does not test a change of population at any effect size: a
larger drop would say only that the generator puts more of its fraud into counterparty structure.
The specification's other ablations of the production ensemble change AUC by less than the card-style
one, each given with its paired account-clustered interval: without agent features by \nAblAgentD{} \nAblAgentDiffci{}, without the corridor
feature by \nAblCorridorD{} \nAblCorridorDiffci{}, without round-sum awareness by \nAblRoundD{}
\nAblRoundDiffci{} (a feature computed from assumed denominations, Section~\ref{sec:limitations}),
without month-end awareness by \nAblMonthD{} \nAblMonthDiffci{}, and without USSD-aware device
handling by \nAblUSSDD{} \nAblUSSDDiffci{}. The agent and corridor intervals lie wholly below no
change, so those differences are detected with the clustering taken into account; the round-sum
interval reaches no change at its upper end, and the month-end and USSD intervals span it. They are
reported as measured on this benchmark, not as evidence of what matters in production.

\subsection{Calibration and explanation}

With equal-width bins the row-weighted calibration error says little at this base rate: on the
diagnostic model, \nBatLowBinRows{} of the \nBatTestRows{} test rows score below
\nBatLowBinTop{}, in the lowest bin, where no decision is made. On the diagnostic model we therefore also
report it in the decision region (score at or above the flag threshold, \nBatDecisionRows{} rows):
\nBatECEDecision{}, against \nBatECEAll{} over all rows.

The production ensemble, measured with the same definitions (the same reliability bins and decision
region), has a higher calibration error than the diagnostic model where decisions are made, on
point estimates with no interval computed, while its row-weighted error is of the same small size. At the gate's training volume its decision region holds
\nGateCalRegionRows{} test rows, \nGateCalRegionFraud{} of them fraud, with an expected calibration
error of \nGateCalRegionECE{}, against \nGateCalECEAll{} over all rows in these equal-width bins (the
gate's own \nGateECE{} uses \nEceBins{} equal-mass bins); at full volume the region holds
\nFvCalRegionRows{} rows (\nFvCalRegionFraud{} fraud) and the error is \nFvCalRegionECE{}, against
\nFvCalECEAll{} over all rows (\texttt{evidence/round2\_gate\_d8083dbc.txt},
\texttt{evidence/round2\_full\_volume\_d8083dbc.txt}). The production model is calibrated by isotonic
regression on its own calibration split, the diagnostic model by Platt scaling (ADR 0029), so the
comparison is between calibration methods as well as between models. The additivity error of the exact TreeSHAP
contributions, which must sum to the model's log-odds output, is \nBatAdditivity{}.

From the smallest configuration to the largest, AUC changes by \nFrontAUCDelta{} as the frontier file
computes it from unrounded values, while the cost of explaining a decision, as a difference of medians, grows between
about \nFrontRatioLow- and \nFrontRatioHigh-fold (the range the rounded medians allow), from
\nFrontShapSmall{} to \nFrontShapLarge{}~ms; prediction alone grows from
\nFrPredA{} to \nFrPredE{}~ms. We infer that the flat accuracy is a property of this easy benchmark;
it should not be generalised. We infer that the latency side is a property of the model and the
explanation algorithm rather than of the data: exact TreeSHAP costs $O(TLD^2)$ for $T$ trees with at
most $L$ leaves and depth $D$ \cite{lundberg2020trees}. The leaves a tree actually grows depend on
the data, and only this benchmark was measured, so that inference is not tested.

The result needs to be kept in proportion. Every configuration explains a decision in about a
millisecond or less at the median on the runner. That is the cost of one booster's
explanation in one process; the serving path's latency against the specification's budget is
\deferred{M10}, so whether explanation cost binds at throughput or at the latency of a single request
is not established here. The ratio depends on the grid: it is measured from the cheapest configuration tried, and a different smallest configuration
would give a different ratio; the growth with trees and depth is the finding, not the number. And
it is one machine's median. If the growth holds on serving hardware, then on data like this the explanation
budget (the latency the specification allows for an explained decision), not the accuracy target, would choose the tree configuration; that is not yet shown.

\textbf{We report the median only.} In the earlier laptop run, \nFrontNegative{} configurations
showed a \emph{negative} explanation cost at the \nPctlTail{}th percentile, which is impossible:
explaining cannot be faster than not explaining. We attributed that tail to scheduler jitter on a
shared machine; the jitter itself was not measured. The runner's tail figures, in the evidence file,
are from a virtual machine on shared hardware, so they are not used either. The
\nLatencyPNineNineShap{}~ms constraint therefore cannot be applied from either run; the
\nPctlTail{}th-percentile frontier on a dedicated machine is \deferred{M10}. The run is also limited to one model family (not the ensemble) and one process (not
the specified multi-process service), and it pays the SHAP cost on every row, whereas in serving it
is paid only on flagged transactions.

\subsection{Operational measures at full volume}
\label{sec:operational}

PB-66 measured the full-volume model where an operator would read it, on the same test period
(\texttt{docs/benchmarks/pb66\_d8083dbc.txt}), each figure beside the best single feature on the
same rows. Like every result of that run, these were specified before it ran and are not blind to a
test period scored before.

\paragraph{Precision and recall in the alert region.} The PR-AUC (area under the precision--recall curve) is \nPbPRAUC{} \nPbPRAUCci{},
against \nPbPRBase{} \nPbPRBaseci{} for the single feature; at this base rate it says more about the
alerts an analyst sees than the AUC does.

\paragraph{An alert budget, assumed.} The specification states no analyst capacity, and the capacity
model its resolution D-10 calls for does not exist, so no single budget can be quoted without
inventing it. Table~\ref{tab:budgets} reads the model at \nBudKA{}, \nBudKB{}, \nBudKC{} and
\nBudKD{} alerts a day, \textbf{all assumed}, over the \nPbDays{} whole days of the test period; the
smallest is below the period's typical daily fraud count, so it measures ranking rather than volume.
Precision is shown beside its cap, the precision a perfect ranking would reach given that
day's frauds, because at the larger budgets the cap, not the model, limits it. The outcomes are
amounts, not costs: the fraud amount the alerts catch and the legitimate amount they hold for review,
in Rwandan francs, with no cost assumed for either.

\textbf{At a tight budget, ties decide the alerts.} At \nBudKA{} alerts a day, \nBudATies{} of the
\nBudAAlerts{} alerts (\nBudATiesPct{}) were decided by ties: the day's last slot fell inside a block of
rows with the same calibrated score, larger than the slots left. We infer that isotonic calibration
causes this, since it maps a whole range of raw scores to one value; the single feature, uncalibrated,
had
\nBaseBudATies{} such alerts at the same budget. At the larger budgets ties decide between
\nBudTiesPctLow{} and \nBudTiesPctHigh{} of alerts. The precision and recall reported at a tie are
expectations under a random choice within the tied block; a deployed tie-break by time, by the
uncalibrated score or by amount would give different figures, and none was evaluated. Many days hold
fewer frauds than the budget, which we infer reflects frauds arriving in bursts: even a perfect ranking would reach a
precision of only \nBudACeil{} at this budget, and the model reaches \nBudAShare{} of that cap.
PB-66 had predicted a precision of at least \nBudPredPrec{} here; it was refuted
(Table~\ref{tab:predictions}). What an operator can take from this, on this benchmark: a queue that
takes the top scores of a calibrated model needs an explicit rule for breaking ties, and a daily budget
should be judged against that day's fraud count, not against a perfect score.

\paragraph{By month (claims register, C-5).} The test period covers the last days of one month, the
whole of the next, and the month after that up to a partial last day. Recall at the flag threshold is
\nMonNovRecall{} \nMonNovRecallci{} in the whole month (\nMonNovFrauds{} frauds) and \nMonDecRecall{}
\nMonDecRecallci{} in most of the last (\nMonDecFrauds{});
the difference, \nMonDiff{} \nMonDiffci{}, includes no change, so no change between the months is
detectable at this size. The partial month (\nMonOctFrauds{} frauds) reads \nMonOctRecall{}
\nMonOctRecallci{}. \textbf{A span this short cannot test C-5's claim of a yearly degradation}. The generator does
vary over time by design (a ramped fraud rate, growing monthly volume, and the novel and reversal
variants confined to the test period), but no part of it models the degradation C-5 claims, so the
claim remains unverified by this benchmark.

\paragraph{Cost curve.} The Drummond--Holte cost curve uses no assumed costs: it plots the normalised
expected cost, minimised over thresholds, against the ratio of the costs of a missed fraud and a false
alarm. Over the range of ratios PB-66 evaluates (\nPbDHLow{} to \nPbDHHigh{} in its probability-cost
terms) the model is below the always-fraud and never-fraud classifiers everywhere; at equal costs its normalised cost is \nPbDHModel{},
against \nPbDHBase{} for the single feature. A normalised curve hides the absolute amounts, which is
why Table~\ref{tab:budgets} gives them.

\paragraph{Reliability by channel.} PB-66 also tabulates predicted against observed fraud rates in
equal-width bins for each channel. They are descriptive, with no prediction attached, and are given
in the evidence file rather than here.
